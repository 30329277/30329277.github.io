The angle on your drawing is not the number on your saw

Every trade hits this: the plan, the sketch or the tube layout gives an angle measured one way, and the mitre saw, cold saw, chop saw, bandsaw table or mitre gauge in front of you is graduated the other way. One reading is from a square cut, another is from the fence line, another is the obtuse counterpart. You convert in your head — and when the two numbers look almost interchangeable, that conversion is exactly where parts die.

🏗 Shop floor & structural crews

Handrails, balustrades, greenhouse frames, site railings. The section drawing gives the included angle; the machine wants something else. One shouting-match later somebody does 90 − 47.5 on a dusty phone keypad.

🔧 Fabrication & motorsport

🪚 Carpenters, joiners & DIY

Angled frames, tapered boxes, picture frames, crown and skirting returns. Some gauges read from square, some read from the fence, and the add-on of 90° switching mid-job is how expensive hardwood turns into offcuts.

Why 40° and 50° are the dangerous pair

40° and 50° are complementary (they add up to 90°), so both look completely reasonable next to the same joint — and they differ by only 10°. Twice that error shows up at the joint, because both ends of the assembly were cut.

It is the worst zone for mental maths:

  • 45° ↔ 45° — the flip is invisible, so nobody notices anything either way.
  • 30° ↔ 60° — far apart, a mistake is usually obvious to the eye.
  • 40° ↔ 50°, 35° ↔ 55°, 47.5° ↔ 42.5° — close enough to look interchangeable, different enough to scrap the part. This is where the errors actually happen.

And it is not only 90°: some layout tasks work with the supplementary reading too, so 180 − a joins the list. Three plausible numbers, one correct setting, one tube.

Frequently Asked Questions

The drawing says 40°, my saw reads 50°. Which one is right?

Both describe the same cut — they are complementary (they add up to 90°). The miter angle a is taken from a square cut, and 90 − a is the same line read the other way. That is exactly why 40/50 trips people up: the two numbers look interchangeable, but setting the wrong one puts 10° of error on each end and 20° at the joint. The app prints both readings side by side so you never have to derive the second one on the shop floor.

Do I set the angle or its complement on the saw?

It depends on how your machine's scale is graduated. If the scale reads 0° at square, set the miter angle a. If it reads 90° at square (fence-line graduation), set 90 − a. Some protractors and layout tasks use the obtuse reading, 180 − a. In Live View use the Original + Complementary / Original + Supplementary button to label the drawing the same way your scale is printed, then always prove it on an offcut first.

How is the miter angle defined?

The miter angle is measured from a square cut, so a square cut is 0°. The live view also shows the complementary (90° − angle) or supplementary values, so you can match the scale printed on your saw.

What is the difference between a pointed cut and a V‑notch cut?

A pointed cut tapers the tube end to a single point and is used where one tube meets another at an angle. A V‑notch (fish‑mouth) cut removes a V‑shaped saddle so the tube wraps a round post or another tube — the classic joint for handrails, T‑joints and roll cages. The app handles both, and the pointed / V‑notch combination.

How do I switch between millimetres and inches?

Tap the gear icon on the home screen and choose Metric or Imperial. Existing values are converted automatically and the choice is remembered.

How accurate are the results?

The maths is exact for ideal geometry; real accuracy depends on how well your inputs match the material — measured diameter, wall thickness, joint angle and kerf. Always cut one first‑off part and check the fit before batch cutting.

Safety Note

Results are setup aids. Before every batch: confirm which way your scale is graduated (0° at square or 90° at square), then confirm end identity, top / bottom face, blunt‑edge side, scrap side and kerf, and check one first‑off part. Especially with near-symmetric pairs such as 40° / 50°, a quick test cut costs a minute; the wrong one costs the tube. Wear eye and hearing protection, and follow your machine’s manual.
